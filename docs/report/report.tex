\begin{abstract}
% Context
Pose-graph LiDAR SLAM corrects the drift of scan-matching odometry by adding loop-closure constraints and
re-optimizing the graph. The information matrix attached to each constraint decides how a correction is spread
along the trajectory, yet many open-source systems use fixed or identity matrices.
% Problem
In the open-source system KISS-SLAM, loop closure improves global consistency but can make the trajectory
locally less accurate than the odometry it starts from, even when every accepted closure is correct. This report
asks whether KISS-SLAM's identity information matrices, which weight one radian of rotation like one metre of
translation, explain that loss, and whether a single fixed rotation weight corrects it on held-out data and on a
second dataset.
% Method
Every prediction and acceptance rule was written down before the corresponding run, and data were split into
tuning, validation and test sets, with the test set run once. On KITTI, the closures were audited against ground
truth, the loss was localized by ablation, and a rotation-weighted information matrix
$\mathrm{diag}(1,1,1,W,W,W)$ was selected on the tuning sequences. The same weight, not re-tuned, was then
evaluated on the NCLT dataset (different sensor, platform and environment), with three runs per configuration
because KISS-SLAM is not repeatable there.
% Results
On KITTI, default loop closure reduces ATE by up to 74\% but raises the KITTI translation error by up to 51\%;
all 14 audited closures are more accurate than the odometry they replace, and disabling closures removes
86--96\% of the loss. With $W=10^3$, tuning sequence~05 goes from 0.517\% to 0.333\% (odometry 0.342\%) and ATE
from 0.66\,m to 0.36\,m; on held-out sequence~08 the local error falls from 0.954\% to 0.841\% and ATE from
2.52\,m to 2.13\,m (odometry 2.27\,m). On NCLT the pre-registered criterion fails: mean local error on the test
sessions falls by 4.0\%, but mean ATE rises by 14.9\% on one session. The rotation weight therefore explains
and corrects the loss on KITTI but does not transfer to NCLT as a reliable improvement.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\paragraph{Context.} A LiDAR SLAM system usually has two parts: a front-end that registers consecutive scans
into an odometry estimate, and a back-end that stores poses and constraints in a graph and optimizes them when
a loop closure is detected~\cite{g2o,gtsam}. Each constraint carries an information matrix (the inverse of its
covariance) that tells the optimizer how much to trust each of its six degrees of freedom. The choice of these
matrices matters most when a loop closure disagrees with accumulated odometry: the optimizer has to distribute
the disagreement, and the information matrices decide where.

\paragraph{Problem.} KISS-SLAM~\cite{kissslam} combines KISS-ICP odometry~\cite{kissicp} with density-map
loop closures~\cite{mapclosures} and pose-graph optimization. Every factor in its graph uses an identity
information matrix. On KITTI~\cite{kitti}, its loop closures reduce global error substantially but increase
local error on every sequence with several closures, by 51\% on sequence~05 (Section~\ref{sec:baselines}).
The expected cause would be wrong closures; an audit shows the closures are accurate. This leaves an
unexplained loss in a system that is widely used as a simple baseline.

\paragraph{Research question.} This report is organized around one question:
\begin{quote}
\emph{Does the relative weighting of rotation and translation in KISS-SLAM's pose-graph information matrices
explain the local accuracy lost to loop closure, and does a single fixed rotation weight correct that loss on
held-out data and on a second dataset recorded with a different sensor and platform?}
\end{quote}

\paragraph{Hypotheses.} The question is broken into four hypotheses, each tested against a prediction and an
acceptance rule fixed before the relevant runs (Section~\ref{sec:setup}). The wording of the research question
and of H1--H4 was written afterwards to organize this report; the predictions they summarize, and the rules
they are judged by, are those recorded in the decision log before each experiment.
\begin{description}
  \item[H1 (effect).] With KISS-SLAM's default identity weights, loop closure increases local error
        relative to the system's own odometry, while reducing global error.
  \item[H2 (mechanism).] The loss is caused by how corrections are distributed under identity weighting, not
        by inaccurate closures, by the odometry front-end, or by early termination of the optimizer.
  \item[H3 (fix on KITTI).] Weighting the rotational block of every information matrix by a constant $W$,
        chosen on the KITTI tuning sequences, removes most of the loss without increasing global error, and
        does not make validation or held-out test sequences worse.
  \item[H4 (transfer to NCLT).] The same $W$, not re-tuned, lowers the mean KITTI translation error over the
        NCLT test sessions without raising any session's mean ATE by more than 10\% (pre-registered as P2.1d).
\end{description}
Table~\ref{tab:verdicts} in Section~\ref{sec:discussion} gives the verdict on each.

\paragraph{Contributions.} The work does not propose a new method: weighting rotation more heavily than
translation is common engineering practice, and uncertainty-aware weights for KISS-ICP-based SLAM have recently
been published by the KISS-SLAM authors~\cite{retroslam} (Section~\ref{sec:related}). What it adds is:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item a measurement, with a tuning/validation/test split, showing that default loop closure in KISS-SLAM
        trades local for global accuracy on KITTI even when every closure is accurate;
  \item a step-by-step diagnosis that rules out closure errors, the odometry front-end and the optimizer's
        iteration limit, and identifies identity weighting as the mechanism;
  \item a pre-registered evaluation of a fixed rotation weight on KITTI, including a held-out sequence;
  \item a negative transfer result: the same weight does not pass its pre-registered criterion on NCLT, a result
        reported as it came out.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Structure.} Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work, Section~\ref{sec:setup} describes the
experimental setup, Sections~\ref{sec:baselines}--\ref{sec:nclt} test H1--H4, Section~\ref{sec:discussion}
answers the research question, and Sections~\ref{sec:limits}--\ref{sec:conclusion} give limitations and
conclusions. A side finding on the Gazebo LiDAR simulator, outside the research question, is in
Appendix~\ref{app:gazebo}.

\section{Literature review}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Pose-graph optimization and noise models.} Pose-graph SLAM is solved as nonlinear least squares in
frameworks such as g2o~\cite{g2o} and GTSAM~\cite{gtsam}; each factor's residual is weighted by its information
matrix. The standard noise model for pose graphs has separate rotational and translational precisions, as made
explicit in SE-Sync~\cite{sesync}, so the rotation-to-translation ratio studied here is a named parameter of the
standard model rather than an implementation detail. Huang et al.~\cite{huang2010} show that pose-graph SLAM
becomes a linear problem once orientations are known, so the nonlinearity, and much of the difficulty, comes from
rotation. Zhang et al.~\cite{degeneracy} show that the information available in each direction depends on scene
geometry, which suggests that no single fixed ratio can be right in every environment.

\paragraph{The KISS family and its uncertainty-aware successor.} KISS-ICP~\cite{kissicp} is a point-to-point ICP
odometry with few parameters; MapClosures~\cite{mapclosures} detects loops from point-cloud density maps; and
KISS-SLAM~\cite{kissslam} combines the two with a pose graph whose factors all use identity information
matrices. Closest to this work, Gupta et al.~\cite{retroslam}, from the same group, replace KISS-ICP's identity
odometry information with an estimate from a sampled local Hessian of the registration cost and add retroactive
loop closure. Their ablation shows large ATE gains over a variant without uncertainty estimation on some
sequences (HeLiPR, Apollo) and none on others (Newer College). They do not evaluate on KITTI or NCLT, do not state
which fixed weight their ablation used, and do not test whether the estimated covariances are consistent. The
claim that uncertainty-aware weights beat identity weights in a KISS-ICP pose graph therefore belongs to them;
this report studies the cruder question of how much a single fixed ratio achieves, under a held-out protocol.

\paragraph{Fixed rotation weights in practice.} Weighting rotation more heavily than translation with hand-picked
constants is ordinary practice in established LiDAR SLAM systems. In LIO-SAM~\cite{liosam} the odometry factors
use variances of $10^{-6}$ for rotation and $10^{-4}$ for translation (rotation information 100 times larger),
while loop closures use an isotropic variance, as KISS-SLAM does. Cartographer~\cite{cartographer} weights loop
closures with separate translation ($1.1\times10^4$) and rotation ($10^5$) weights, and
hdl\_graph\_slam~\cite{hdlgraphslam} maps the scan-matching fitness to separate translation and rotation standard
deviations. These values come from the projects' source configurations; none of the projects publishes an
evaluation of the choice. The value used here ($W=10^3$ in g2o's convention) is at the high end of these ratios.
The idea of weighting rotation more is therefore not new; what this report adds is a controlled, pre-registered
measurement of it on one system.

\paragraph{Estimating registration covariance.} The principled alternative to fixed weights is to estimate the
covariance of each registration. Censi~\cite{censi2007} derived a closed-form ICP covariance from the cost
Hessian. Later work showed that such estimates are overconfident, because they ignore initialization error and
real-world effects: Brossard et al.~\cite{brossard2020} add an initialization term, and CELLO-3D~\cite{cello3d}
learns the covariance from data. Learning-based visual odometry also benefits from well-modelled
covariances~\cite{macvo}, and SuperLoc~\cite{superloc} predicts alignment risk for LiDAR-inertial localization.
On consistency, R{\o}nning et al.~\cite{ronning2026} find that the covariances reported by LiDAR-inertial filters
are two to seven orders of magnitude too tight, and argue for proper scoring rules rather than error metrics
alone. The KISS-SLAM maintainers have given a related reason for shipping identity weights: the Gauss-Newton
Hessian of point-to-point ICP is unreliable, and better estimators were too slow.

\paragraph{Robust back-ends.} Switchable constraints~\cite{switchable}, dynamic covariance
scaling~\cite{dcs} and graduated non-convexity~\cite{gnc} reweight or reject loop closures that are outliers.
They address wrong closures. Since every closure audited here is accurate, they target a different failure,
although a robust kernel could still change how inlier corrections are distributed; this was not tested.

\paragraph{Position of this work.} No published work was found that evaluates a fixed rotation-to-translation
ratio on KISS-SLAM with a held-out split, or that reports what happens when a weight chosen on KITTI is moved to
NCLT. Both are modest, diagnostic contributions. The search behind this section (about 30 web searches and
source checks, November 2026) is documented in the repository; a paper doing the same under different wording
may still exist.

\paragraph{Software.} KISS-ICP~1.3.0 and KISS-SLAM~0.0.2, each in its own \texttt{pipx} environment, evaluated with
\texttt{evo}~1.37.1~\cite{evo}. KISS-SLAM solves its pose graph with g2o~\cite{g2o}. Two local changes were made,
both kept in the repository: (i) a patch to KISS-SLAM that reads the diagonal of the odometry and loop-closure
information matrices from environment variables and falls back to \texttt{np.eye(6)} when they are unset (checked
to reproduce the unpatched output exactly on sequence~05); (ii) a one-line fix to KISS-ICP's TUM-format export,
which fails on recent NumPy versions and does not affect the estimated poses. Two undeclared dependencies were
added to the \texttt{pipx} environments. The NCLT GTSAM reconstruction in Section~\ref{sec:nclt} uses
GTSAM~4.3.0~\cite{gtsam}.

\paragraph{Hardware and operating system.} All runs used one laptop (Dell G15: 20 CPU threads, 32\,GB RAM; the
pipelines run on the CPU, and the RTX~4060 GPU is not used). The KITTI experiments and the NCLT tuning and
validation runs used Ubuntu~24.04 with Python~3.12. Before the NCLT test runs the laptop was upgraded to
Ubuntu~26.04; the tools were reinstalled at the same pinned versions on a separately managed Python~3.12 and both
patches restored. NumPy changed (to 2.5.4 for KISS-ICP and \texttt{evo}, 2.2.6 for KISS-SLAM; earlier versions
were not recorded). A recheck rule was fixed before any test run: the new environment was accepted only if
new runs on a validation session fell within the old ranges on both metrics. It passed after six runs per
condition (Section~\ref{sec:nclt}).

\paragraph{Metrics.} ATE is the RMSE of absolute position error after SE(3) alignment (\texttt{evo\_ape -a}) and
measures global consistency. Local accuracy is measured by the KITTI translation error (average over
100--800\,m segments, in percent), with RPE over 100\,m segments (\texttt{evo\_rpe -d 100}) as a cross-check. On
NCLT, a re-implementation of the metrics that tolerates missing ground-truth frames reproduces \texttt{evo} for
ATE and the KITTI metric.

\paragraph{Pre-registration.} Before every experiment, the hypothesis, the predicted outcome and the acceptance
rule were committed to a dated decision log in the repository. Results were recorded whether or not they
confirmed the prediction, and rules were not relaxed afterwards; later corrections to earlier entries are logged
as corrections.

\paragraph{Run-to-run noise and comparison rule.} KISS-ICP is repeatable: on KITTI a parallel rerun reproduced
every metric exactly, and on NCLT repeated runs differ by at most $10^{-11}$\,m (repeatable to rounding error, not
bit-identical). KISS-SLAM is not fully repeatable. On KITTI, six default runs were identical on 05, 06, 07 and
08--10, but on 00 ATE varied by 1.1\% and on 02 by 3.3\% (the number of accepted closures flips between 1 and 2);
differences inside these bands are treated as noise. On NCLT the spread is much larger (Section~\ref{sec:nclt}),
so each configuration is run three times and a difference counts only if the three-run ranges do not overlap.

\section{H2: diagnosis}
Each step below tested one explanation for the local-accuracy loss, with the prediction fixed in advance.

\paragraph{Are the closures wrong?} No. For each of the 14 closures accepted on 00, 02 and 05, the relative pose
was compared with ground truth and with the relative pose implied by odometry between the same scans. Every
closure was more accurate than the odometry chain it replaces (closure errors 0.12--4.88\,m against
0.91--18.1\,m), and the overlap score used for acceptance did not visibly predict closure error.

\paragraph{Is it the odometry inside KISS-SLAM?} No. With closures disabled (overlap threshold 1.0), KISS-SLAM
matches standalone odometry: KITTI error 0.349\% on 05 (odometry 0.342\%, default 0.517\%), 0.532\% on 00 and
0.543\% on 02. Between 86\% and 96\% of the gap appears only when closures are applied.

\paragraph{Is the optimizer stopping early?} No. Raising the iteration limit from 10 to 50 or 100 left 05
unchanged to six digits.

\paragraph{Where do the corrections go?} For each 100\,m segment we compared the change introduced by the
optimizer with the segment's odometry error. The correlation between correction size and odometry error was
0.13 (05), $-$0.11 (00) and 0.23 (02); on 05 the root-mean-square correction per segment (0.49\,m) was about
seven times larger than the evenly distributed drift (about 0.07\,m). Removing three of the five closures on
05 shrank the corrections in the affected span by 91\% and restored odometry-level local accuracy, but the
same intervention had no effect on 00 and 02, so closure selection alone is not a general explanation.

\paragraph{Mechanism.} In the KISS-SLAM source, every factor (between local maps, loop closures, and the final
per-scan refinement) is added with \texttt{np.eye(6)}. Odometry over 100\,m drifts on the order of 0.5\,m in
translation but only about 0.1$^\circ$ (0.0017\,rad) in heading, so a physically consistent weighting would make
rotation roughly $(0.5/0.0017)^2 \approx 10^5$ times more expensive than an identity matrix does. With identity
weights, the optimizer can satisfy a closure by bending headings almost for free, distributing the correction
where the odometry was in fact accurate. The diagnosis rules out the three alternative causes named in H2 and is
consistent with this mechanism; the direct test of the mechanism is whether changing the weights removes the
loss, which is H3.

\section{H3: rotation weighting on KITTI}
\label{sec:fix}
The rotational block is weighted by $W$: $\Lambda=\mathrm{diag}(1,1,1,W,W,W)$, applied to all factors through the
patch described in Section~\ref{sec:setup}.

\paragraph{Round 1 (00, 02, 05).} Weighting the last three entries by $10^4$ reduced 05's KITTI error to
0.341\%; weighting the first three by $10^4$ instead increased it to 0.725\%, which identifies the last block as
rotation. The prediction that only one of the two variants would matter was wrong: only the ratio between the
blocks matters, so the second variant made rotation cheaper still. Uniformly down-weighting closures by 10
also helped 05 (0.392\%) but raised 02's ATE by 17\%, outside its noise band.

\paragraph{Weight selection (00--05).} The selection rule, fixed in advance, picked the $W\in\{10^3,10^4,10^5\}$
with the largest mean improvement in KITTI error, subject to no sequence losing more than 20\% ATE or 5\% KITTI
error. Mean changes were $-5.5\%$, $-4.7\%$ and $-4.7\%$; $W=10^3$ was selected. Loop-free sequences 01, 03 and
04 were unchanged. On 05, $W=10^3$ gives 0.333\% (odometry 0.342\%) and ATE 0.364\,m (default 0.661\,m); the cost
on 00 is +1.9\%. The gain was already saturated at the smallest weight tested, so the optimum may lie lower;
this was not explored to avoid post-hoc tuning.

\paragraph{Validation and test.} Table~\ref{tab:test} reports the single runs on validation and test sequences.
Both validation sequences pass the pre-set rule. On the held-out sequence 08, default loop closure made the
trajectory worse than odometry in both metrics; with $W=10^3$ it becomes better than odometry in ATE and loses
only 2.6\% in local accuracy. Sequences 09 and 10 have no accepted closures and are unchanged. Repeated default
runs on 08--10 were identical, so the 08 difference is not run-to-run noise.

\section{H4: replication on NCLT}
\label{sec:nclt}
\paragraph{Why and how.} KITTI offers few loops and one platform. The comparison was repeated on the University
of Michigan North Campus Long-Term dataset (NCLT)~\cite{nclt}, using the split in Table~\ref{tab:data}.
$W=10^3$ was taken from KITTI and \emph{not} re-tuned. Because NCLT ground truth comes from a LiDAR and RTK-GPS
graph, it partly shares the LiDAR being evaluated.

\paragraph{Noise.} Unlike on KITTI, KISS-SLAM is far from repeatable on NCLT: with the same number of accepted
closures, ATE ranged from 2.0 to 6.4\,m on 2012-04-29. Repeated KISS-ICP runs agree to about $10^{-11}$\,m on the
four sessions checked, so the spread arises inside KISS-SLAM, whose own odometry also varies between runs. Every
configuration was therefore run three times, and a difference counts only if the three-run ranges do not overlap.
A six-run recheck on 2012-05-26 after the operating-system upgrade showed that three runs had underestimated the
spread, and one validation claim was retracted.

\paragraph{Tuning and validation (H1 on NCLT).} Default loop closure lowered ATE on 8 of 9 tuning runs, but raised
local error clearly on only one session (2012-04-29, all three runs), against two of three predicted; H1, as
registered for NCLT, is not met, and the KITTI effect does not transfer cleanly. On 2012-04-29, $W=10^3$ lowered
both metrics beyond noise, as predicted (Table~\ref{tab:nclt}). Both validation sessions passed the pre-set rule
(not worse beyond noise).

\paragraph{Test.} H4 required $W=10^3$ to lower the mean KITTI metric over the test sessions without raising any
session's mean ATE by more than 10\%. The first part holds (1.674\% to 1.606\%, lower on all three sessions); the
second fails on 2012-06-15, where mean ATE rose by 14.9\% (2.03 to 2.34\,m), a small absolute change on a session
where the default happened to be very repeatable. On 2013-04-05 the weighted runs were far more spread than the
default ones (ATE 4.9--14.4\,m). The criterion is therefore not met: rotation weighting chosen on KITTI does not
transfer to NCLT as a reliable improvement, although it does not clearly hurt local accuracy and may help it
slightly. The KISS-SLAM maintainers have suggested that NCLT's Segway, which rotates non-uniformly, may violate
the constant-velocity initial guess of the odometry; this explanation has not been tested here.

\paragraph{A unit convention.} Rebuilding KISS-SLAM's pose graph in GTSAM~\cite{gtsam} showed that g2o's SE(3) edge
measures rotation error by the quaternion vector, about half the angle. The weight $W=10^3$ is therefore $250$ on
the rotation angle; the physical estimate of Section~5 ($\approx 10^5$ on the angle) corresponds to
$\approx 4\times 10^5$ in g2o's convention. With this conversion, GTSAM reproduces all 18 KISS-SLAM tuning graphs
to within 1\,cm.

\section{Discussion: answer to the research question}
\label{sec:discussion}

\centering
\small

\midrule

     KITTI: supported (00, 02, 05, 06, 08) \\

The answer to the research question is split. On KITTI, identity weighting explains most of the local accuracy
lost to loop closure, and a single fixed rotation weight corrects it, including on a held-out sequence. On NCLT,
the effect itself is weaker and the same weight does not pass its pre-registered test. The literature offers a
reason to expect this: the information available in each direction depends on scene geometry and
motion~\cite{degeneracy}, so a constant ratio tuned on a car in Karlsruhe need not suit a Segway on a campus, and
the gains of geometry-dependent weights in Gupta et al.~\cite{retroslam} are themselves large on some datasets
and absent on others. This reading is an interpretation, not something the experiments here tested.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}
The KITTI results come from one sensor and one SLAM system; on NCLT the fix did not pass its test criterion, so
the KITTI improvement should not be read as general. The KITTI effect is large on two sequences (05 and 08) and
small or absent elsewhere; the local loss on 06 is not recovered. $W$ was chosen from three candidates and
the information matrix is a scaled identity, not a calibrated covariance. KISS-SLAM is not fully repeatable on
some KITTI sequences and much less so on NCLT, where three runs per configuration proved too few on one session.
The research question and hypotheses were phrased after the experiments, although each prediction and rule they
summarize was registered beforehand. A robust back-end was not tested as an alternative explanation, and the
fixed weight has not yet been compared with the Hessian-based weights of Gupta et al.~\cite{retroslam} on the same
data.

\section{Conclusion and future work}
\label{sec:conclusion}
Identity information matrices let a pose-graph optimizer absorb loop-closure corrections as cheap heading changes,
which costs local accuracy even when every closure is accurate. Weighting rotation consistently with the physical
scale of odometry error removes most of that cost on the affected KITTI sequences, including a held-out one,
while improving global consistency. On NCLT, a single fixed weight chosen on KITTI lowers local error slightly but
does not reliably keep global accuracy, which points to weights that depend on the factor rather than one constant.
The next pre-registered step compares identity weights, the fixed weight and the ICP-Hessian weights of Gupta et
al.~\cite{retroslam} on the same NCLT tuning sessions, to measure how much of a geometry-dependent weighting's
gain a single constant recovers. Further steps are evaluation on datasets with more loops and more degenerate
scenes~\cite{superloc}, consistency tests of the resulting covariances~\cite{ronning2026}, and a multi-sensor
extension on NCLT, which is in progress and not reported here.

every prediction and result are available at \url{https://github.com/yassinmtibaa/lidar-loop-closure-weighting}.

\section{Side finding: a range artifact in Gazebo's GPU LiDAR}
\label{app:gazebo}
This finding comes from earlier work on simulated sensor noise and is outside the research question. To model
sensor noise for simulation, range noise was measured on KITTI by second differences along each beam on road
surface, an estimator verified to within 1\% on synthetic white noise. It gives 0.84--0.90\,cm at 3--10\,m and
1.65--1.83\,cm at 30--40\,m (sequences 00, 02, 05), an upper bound on white range noise. Plane-fit residuals, by
contrast, are 4--8\,cm and measure road shape rather than the sensor.

In Gazebo Sim~8 (Harmonic), a 64-beam \texttt{gpu\_lidar} over a flat ground plane showed two artifacts. A
360$^\circ$ configuration returned spurious points 1--1.6\,m from the sensor over most azimuths; a 120$^\circ$
sector did not. Within the clean sector, the ideal (noise-free) ranges contain deterministic jumps: about 40\% of
ground triples include a step, 88\% of steps appear as adjacent opposite-sign pairs, and the step size is about
0.19\% of range beyond 8\,m. Changing the sensor's minimum range had no effect. The resulting second-difference
spread (2.6\,cm at 9\,m, 15\,cm at 29\,m) exceeds the real sensor noise beyond about 8\,m, by roughly ten times
at 30\,m, so adding a realistic noise model on top of this sensor does not produce realistic data at range. The
cause of the jumps was not identified. A noise-injection ROS~2 node was implemented and verified (injected and
measured noise agree within 2\%). These findings concern one Gazebo version and a flat-ground statistic.
